# Listening Again: The Right to Revise

Originally 13 October 2026. Revised 19 October 2026 following the Author’s CTM,
Storywalker and music-making discussions, including the Author-selected IOANA
passages, a verified Ableton study and hand-shaped playback of its corrected render
in XR, with an optional response to the surrounding soundscape. Working artistic
direction; this is neither a submitted proposal nor a report of research findings.

Implementation status below describes local development as of 19 October. It
does not establish that the corresponding software has merged into the repository.

## Central proposition

**Listening Again explores how an author can reshape both a musical fragment and
the interpretation an AI places around it, using hands, sound and immersive space.**

A song can become attached to a place, a period or a possible reading of oneself.
Hearing it later does not require that reading to remain fixed. Sampling makes
this revisability tangible: a voice can be isolated, repeated, stretched,
interrupted or brought forward in a new arrangement. Storywalker asks how the
meaning proposed around that sound can remain equally open to revision.

The traveller is the Author; the AI is the Editor. The Editor may offer a
connection, suggest a cut or help build a tool. The Author chooses what becomes
audible, what wording belongs beside it and what remains private or unresolved.
Trust has to be rehearsed through those choices, corrections and returns.

**Working question:** What changes when a listener becomes the maker of a new
version of a selected musical fragment, while retaining the right to contest the story
an AI tells about it?

## From The Noise Era to deliberate composition

The Author’s public essay, [The Noise Era — or Why I Think We’re All Broadcasting
Now](https://substack.com/@adinablaze/p-183960256), supplies an entry point. It
recounts hearing Romanian music in Copenhagen and the feeling that an external
soundscape had answered something internal. The encounter raises a question about
inference, coincidence and the experience of being addressed. It does not establish
how the music was selected or that a system could access the listener’s thoughts.

The new musical study moves toward deliberate composition: the Author selects a
fragment, works on its sound and decides what relationship to make audible. The
Copenhagen account concerns Dragonu; the present source choice is IOANA’s
**“Acasă, Oriunde,” from I Mare de la Ioana**. These are distinct encounters and
sources. Their relationship is a present authored proposal, not an identity or
causal connection discovered in listening data.

“Home,” recognition, movement and belonging may become questions for the work.
They are not meanings assigned in advance from the song title, language or a
travel trace. The Author may supply another reading or leave the relationship open.

## One musical scene

The bounded musical study now exists as a private **Ableton Live** set and rendered
loop, with selected IOANA passages and an original **148 BPM heavy industrial /
acid-techno** arrangement. Boston 168 is the Author’s reference for acid energy
and texture.
The piece should be worth listening to as music, with room for experiment,
pleasure, friction and decisions that do not need an autobiographical explanation.

1. **Hear the fragment.** Choose the passage together, identify its recording and
   retain its provenance. Decide what the listener should be able to recognise.
2. **Make a version.** Work with cuts, repetition, acid movement, distorted kick,
   rumble and metallic percussion. Compare what happens when the voice becomes
   rhythmic material and when it returns to the foreground.
3. **Contest a reading.** The Editor offers a clearly labelled, tentative reading.
   The Author can accept, rewrite, refuse or leave it unresolved. Musical edits
   remain available independently of agreement with that reading.
4. **Listen again.** Keep the earlier arrangement and dated response. Return later
   to make another version or retain the first. A changed interpretation does not
   erase what was previously heard or said.

The correction from the earlier rhythm suggestions to a heavier direction and
148 BPM is an actual formative exchange in this development. It demonstrates a
specific Author decision changing a sketch; it is not evidence that the wider
research method has succeeded.

Accessibility remains a method of authorship. Intensity can be chosen and adjusted;
quietness is not a prerequisite for access. Playback is explicit, with volume,
pause and stop controls, and text sufficient to understand the study. Refusing an
interpretation must not remove control of the music or compel personal disclosure.

## Shaping sound with hands and the surrounding room

The XR instrument takes inspiration from Hannah Eikens’s
[Tabula](https://hannah.eikens.com/projects/tabula), described on its exhibition
placard as a deformable interface for musical expressivity. The reference is that
premise; the project’s sensing and sound mappings have not been verified here.
The following mappings describe the artistic direction. The first browser
implementation and its live trials are distinguished below.

A suspended ribbon is the starting form for the current instrument. The longer-term
direction imagines a soft surface carrying the chosen fragment, with independently
playable layers and a third compression gesture. These richer mappings remain
proposals beyond the current tone/stretch and fold/echo controls:

| Action | Proposed sound response | Proposed spatial response |
|---|---|---|
| Stretch | Extend the fragment into a sustained texture | Spread the sound and form farther around the listener |
| Fold | Overlap fragments into rhythmic layers | Gather those layers close together |
| Compress, as a later addition | Increase density and distortion within chosen limits | Contract the form around the sound |

Keep the original heavy industrial / acid-techno groove at **148 BPM**. Independent
shaping of the fragment above that pulse remains a next experiment. A virtual surface
does not provide physical resistance; a real flexible object could be explored
later if touch proves essential to the instrument.

The surrounding soundscape can become a second, optional influence on the form:
the low rumble of public transport, the changing bustle of a coffee shop or music
chosen for a listening session. Start with one audible quality, such as the rise
and fall of sound level, gently rippling the surface. Later experiments might let
low-frequency sound create broad movement and sharper sounds create finer
movement. These are artistic mappings to test, not interpretations of a place,
a song’s meaning or the listener’s mood. Hearing bass through a microphone does
not establish a measurement of physical vibration.

The Author chooses the input, its influence and when to freeze or switch it off.
Hand edits remain available throughout. Ambient influence should be adjustable
and bounded so a loud room cannot unexpectedly overwhelm the composition. Its
response remains distinguishable from the Author’s deliberate gestures; neither
counts as accepting an AI reading. The first version keeps the tempo fixed even
as its texture and virtual form respond.

Live sound input requires an explicit start and a visible listening state, with
an immediate stop. The proposed default is temporary local sound analysis, without
retaining audio or transcribing nearby speech. Replaying the input later would
require a separately chosen recording; a saved arrangement alone cannot recover
the original room. A chosen music source and any sound from the installation’s
own speakers need a clear input boundary so the system does not unintentionally
keep reacting to its own output. None of this enables a microphone or records
sound through the current documentation change.

This connects the instrument to **Listening Again: The Right to Revise**. The
Author can keep a version, revisit the same fragment in a different soundscape,
and decide whether a new arrangement changes its present meaning. A comparison
should identify whether the musical source, hand edits or ambient influence
changed, rather than assigning the difference to the environment alone. Earlier
versions remain available; embodied musical choices and editorial agreement
remain separate.

## Verified musical and XR rehearsal — 19 October 2026

The Author confirmed [the official IOANA recording](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=woR21YYig0A)
and selected **00:26–00:30** and a longer **00:35–00:46** passage, preserving the
ending of a word. The beat drop at **00:42** became the chosen anchor. The Author
had rejected an earlier transformed cut as the wrong passage; its attribution
remains disputed. The revised play set keeps that cut inactive. Source labels and
successful playback did not substitute for the Author hearing and correcting it.

The revised private Ableton study contains a **16-bar loop at 148 BPM**, editable
chop patterns, the original backing and the two selected source passages. The
main passage has eleven playable slices, with **G** triggering the chosen drop in
the prepared laptop-keyboard mapping. Saturation, filtering and beat-synced delay
provide an editable heavier treatment. The Author confirmed both the intended
section and the keyboard chops in a physical trial. A native Ableton render was
also checked separately for duration, source-layer contribution and clipping.
These checks establish a working production study, rather than a finished track
or an assessment of its artistic quality.

That corrected render was then loaded through **Shape sound · 148 BPM** in the
existing XR space. The loop keeps its playback rate while hand separation shapes
tone/stretch and finger openness shapes folds/echoes. Optional nearby-sound level
adds ripples to the form. The Author confirmed the corrected version worked in
XR; earlier live trials also confirmed hand shaping and nearby-sound response.
These first-person confirmations remain separate from automated mapping and
resource-lifecycle checks.

The current XR instrument shapes the **rendered mix**. Independent control of
individual chops or stems, a live connection to Ableton, saved hand performances
and restoration of a previous interactive version remain future work. Camera and
microphone inputs are temporary; the instrument does not record or save them.
The production files and sampled audio remain private local materials, excluded
from Git and the public demo. This direction records the rehearsal without
authorising publication of its audio.

## What Storywalker contributes

Storywalker provides a place to encounter the versions, inspect their sources and
retain disagreement across time. Its WORLD / TIME / MEMORY structure supports
three distinctions:

- **WORLD:** where an encounter is reported to have happened, with its source and
  limits visible. A studio recording cannot stand in for a live cover or establish
  what played in a particular room. A chosen live sound input may shape the scene
  without identifying its location or establishing who was present.
- **TIME:** recording, encounter, platform observation, composition and later
  review have different dates. Their sequence can be inspected without collapsing
  them into a single story.
- **MEMORY:** a present reading and a musical arrangement can change while earlier
  versions remain available. An accepted reading and a later refusal can coexist.

The recent private-source work gives this question a practical foundation:
selected playlist snapshots, listening records, Calendar plans, companion
recordings and tracker exports can be preserved privately with distinct origins.
Collection and encryption do not author a meaning or grant permission to publish.
A playlist is not a listening event; a Calendar entry is a plan; a recording does
not establish attention, emotion or significance.

BODY and the private HEALTH workspace remain optional context, separately chosen
from artistic use. They do not identify a mental state, explain a musical choice
or generate automatic hormonal interpretations. Personal measurements, account
records and private reflections are outside this public direction and public demo.

## The maker’s position and CTM argument

The maker’s position spans software engineering, live technical production and a
developing music practice. Hybrid screen-based work and the arrival of AI as a
development partner are situated conditions of the work. Festivals, listening and
hands-on production bring timing, material decisions and other people into that
practice. Personal wellbeing reflections remain self-report, held privately;
they do not establish a treatment effect or a general claim about AI.

Years of manual coding remain part of the maker’s judgement. Working with AI raises
the question of how that judgement can shape a collaboration, including when to
correct its suggestions or take over. Live production makes accountability
concrete through timing, shared work and decisions with audible consequences.

The CTM argument is **the right to revise**. **Trust has to be rehearsed** describes
how to test it: the Editor’s assistance must remain open to correction in both the
sound and its framing. Technical capability and the amount of collected data are
insufficient evidence of artistic agency. Making, refusing and returning need
perceptible consequences that the Author can assess.

This develops the 13 October question, **Who Gets to Process? Neurodivergent Access
and the Rejectable Edit**. Pace, disclosure and interpretive authority remain
central; composing now gives those questions a concrete musical practice.
The work does not claim to represent all neurodivergent listeners.

## Next rehearsal and ten-minute presentation

Build from the verified source choices and short arrangement. Compare a restrained
version with the heavier chop treatment, and decide what should remain recognisable
and which transformations are worth keeping. Preserve the source, the rejected cut
and each selected version privately. Public playback requires a separately chosen
and suitable extract with its source-credit and permission basis recorded. The
public demo continues to contain no bundled IOANA audio.

For the next XR rehearsal, compare hand shaping with sliders, and ambient influence
off and on using one deliberately chosen sound source. Review comfort, responsiveness
and the feel of the musical changes, alongside Stop everything, leaving the page
and explicit resumption. The initial live trial does not establish those behaviours
across all devices or situations. Keep input choice, intensity, freeze, playback
and stop understandable without an AI explanation. A later experiment can test
separate control of the fragment and backing before adding more gestures.

A revised ten-minute presentation could move through:

- **0–2 minutes:** the maker’s position and the public essay’s question about an
  external sound seeming to address an internal experience.
- **2–5:** one musical fragment and its transformations, using only material
  selected and suitable for public playback. Show how hands shape it and compare
  the optional ambient influence off and on. The browser instrument is now a working
  prototype; distinguish its verified controls from proposed stem control, saved
  performances and a live Ableton connection.
- **5–7:** one Editor proposal, the Author’s response and what actually changes
  in the listening or its framing. If the interaction is still proposed, say so.
- **7–9:** provenance, access, privacy and what a genuine later return might reveal.
  Include a dated comparison only after that return has happened.
- **9–10:** the research question left open and the next bounded experiment.

The [precedent matrix](precedents-2026-10-13.md) remains useful for infrastructure,
situated archives and permission to speak. These are our adaptations of those
researchers’ lenses, not their endorsements. Liz Pelly’s *Mood Machine* remains a
reading in progress; refer to an actual passage only after reading it.

No participant outcomes, longitudinal findings, clinical efficacy or completed
submission follow from this direction. Private sessions and recordings remain
private until separately selected for release. Artistic readiness, public audio
release, software security review and merge authorisation remain separate decisions.
